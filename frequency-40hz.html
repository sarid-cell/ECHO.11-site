What is a binaural beat?
Two slightly different tones — one per ear. Your brain detects the difference and produces a third tone that doesn't physically exist. An auditory illusion with measurable neurological effects, first documented by physicist Heinrich Wilhelm Dove in 1839.
Why 40 Hz?
40Hz sits inside the Gamma band (30–80Hz) — the range the brain shows during focused attention, working memory, and the binding of separate senses into one coherent moment. Fast, precise, awake.
What happens in the brain?
The brain leans toward the frequency it perceives — a process called neural entrainment. As you listen, gamma-range activity is encouraged while the sound plays. Attention narrows onto the one task in front of you.
Can it help with focus?
Gamma-band activity is associated with sustained attention and working memory. This session was built as a free deep-work companion: press play, put one task in front of you, and give it 11 undistracted minutes. It is a self-care practice, not a medical treatment, and response varies from person to person.

Research Inspiration · The 40Hz Question

40Hz is one of the most actively studied frequencies in modern neuroscience. Research groups — most visibly at MIT's Picower Institute — have spent the last decade probing what happens when the brain is driven at gamma rhythm with light and sound.

That work is early, much of it in animal models, and none of it studied this recording. This experience is inspired by that research. It is an open scientific question, not a clinical trial.

What the research actually shows

Not a smart drug. A metronome.

Across gamma-frequency research, the honest summary is narrower than the headlines: steady 40Hz stimulation can nudge the brain's own rhythms while the sound is playing, and listeners often report a state of alert, single-task attention. Evidence for lasting cognitive change is early and mixed. Treat this session as a metronome for your attention — something to work alongside — not something that upgrades the hardware.

Research findings, not medical claims. Nothing here treats or prevents any condition. Read the full disclaimer.
The 11-minute threshold
Research on neural entrainment suggests that 7–15 minutes is the window in which brainwave synchronization stabilizes. 11 minutes is the session length ECHO.11 uses across every frequency — long enough to settle in, short enough to fit before the work. The number sits at the core of ECHO.11's design philosophy.
The full disclaimer

ECHO.11 is a self-care tool, not a medical device, and nothing here is medical advice. The 40Hz session is not a diagnosis, a treatment, or a substitute for care from a doctor, therapist, or psychiatrist. If anxiety, pain, or sleeplessness is disrupting your life, please talk to someone qualified — this can sit alongside that work, but it does not replace it.

Response varies from person to person, and some people notice nothing at all. That is a normal outcome, not a failure on your part.

A few practical limits. Do not listen while driving or operating anything that needs your attention — the session is built for stillness with your eyes closed. If you have epilepsy or a seizure disorder, or you are fitted with a pacemaker or a cochlear implant, check with your doctor before using audio entrainment of any kind. Keep the volume low; the effect comes from the frequency, not the loudness. If a session leaves you dizzy, disoriented, or more agitated than when you started, stop and take the headphones off.

The research described on this page is drawn from published work on binaural beats and neural entrainment. It reports what studies have observed in general — not what will happen to you, and no efficacy figures are claimed for this specific recording.

This is not a crisis service. If you are in danger or thinking about harming yourself, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line right away.
