Is this a binaural beat?
No. Both ears hear exactly the same sound. A binaural beat only forms when each ear gets a different tone, which means headphones — and earbuds in bed shift and press every time you turn over. No study has tested sleeping in earbuds, and everyday earphone use has been linked to more bacteria in the ear canal. This one is built for a speaker on the bedside table.
Then why is it called Delta?
Delta (0.5–4 Hz) is the brain's own rhythm in the deepest stage of sleep. You can't enter it while awake, and no sound plays it into you. The name marks where you're headed. The session's job is the stretch before: lower arousal, a steady sound that covers the room, slow breathing — and then it gets out of the way.
What you'll hear
Low-passed brown noise under a soft open fifth — 110, 165 and 220 Hz, tuned to exact ratios so the tones never beat against each other. Both rise and settle on a ten-second cycle. After 14 minutes the tone recedes and only the noise is left; from minute 22 everything fades, and the last seconds are silence.
Why six breaths a minute?
It's the pace used in heart-rate-variability biofeedback, where it produces the largest swings in heart rate (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014). In one small study, 20 minutes of it before bed shortened the time to fall asleep in 14 people with insomnia (Tsai et al., 2015). Promising, not proven — and you don't have to follow it. The swell is there if you want something to breathe with.
What should you feel?
Individual response varies. Most commonly reported:
Heaviness Slower breathing Thoughts losing their grip Not hearing the ending Nothing at all (normal)
What the research on sound and sleep actually shows

Not a sleeping pill. A dimmer switch.

Music helps people feel they slept better — the stopwatch is less sure. A 2022 Cochrane review of 13 trials (1,007 adults with insomnia) found moderate-certainty evidence that listening to music improves self-rated sleep quality, and low-certainty evidence that it may ease self-reported trouble falling asleep. In the three studies that measured sleep objectively, it did not clearly shorten the time to fall asleep or lengthen sleep.1 Earlier meta-analyses point the same way, with the benefit showing up on questionnaires rather than instruments.2

Why people reach for it. In a survey of 651 people, 62% said they use music to fall asleep — to relax, and to block out noise and their own thoughts. There was no single "sleep sound": what worked was personal.3

Steady noise is the weakest link. A systematic review of 38 studies rated the evidence that continuous noise improves sleep as very low quality, and warned it may also disturb sleep and hearing.4 A 2022 review of 34 studies likewise found no strong evidence for white or pink noise.5 That is why the noise here stays low, sits under 650 Hz, and switches itself off.

The impressive results come from the lab. When short sounds are timed to the brain's own slow waves during deep sleep — with an EEG reading them in real time — slow waves grow stronger and next-day recall improves, in young adults and in 13 adults aged 60–84.6, 7 A track on a phone can't see your brainwaves, and this session doesn't pretend to.

Delta binaural beats: one tiny study. A 3 Hz binaural beat played during sleep lengthened deep sleep in 8 people.8 A 2023 systematic review of 14 EEG studies found binaural beats entrain brainwaves inconsistently — five studies yes, eight no, one mixed.9 Too thin to build a bedtime on, and it would need headphones.

Research findings, not medical claims. Nothing here treats or prevents any condition. Read the full disclaimer.

What your brain does after you let go

Drifting
N1
Light sleep. Easy to wake; the room still gets through.
Settling
N2
Sleep spindles appear — short bursts that help shut out sound.
Delta
N3
Slow-wave sleep. Large, slow 0.5–4 Hz waves. Hardest to wake.
Dreaming
REM
Vivid dreams. The cycle repeats every 90 minutes or so.
Sorting the day
During slow-wave sleep the brain replays what it learned while awake. The slow oscillations act as a conductor, grouping faster rhythms — spindles and hippocampal ripples — so memories move into longer-term storage (Diekelmann & Born, 2010).10
Rinsing the brain
In mice, sleep widened the space between brain cells by about 60% and sped up the clearance of waste, including β-amyloid (Xie et al., 2013).11 In sleeping humans, each slow wave was followed by a pulse of cerebrospinal fluid through the brain (Fultz et al., 2019).12 That human study shows the rhythm, not the cleaning itself — but the two pictures fit.
So what can a sound do?
Deep sleep is the brain's own work, and it arrives on its own schedule. The most a sound can honestly do is get you to the start line a little calmer, keep the room from pulling you back, and then go quiet. That's the whole design of this session.
How to use it
1. Phone on the bedside table, volume low — just above the sound of the room. No headphones.
2. Lights out, press play, screen down. The audio keeps playing with the screen locked on most phones.
3. Breathe with the swell while you're awake. When you drift, stop following it. At 30 minutes it reaches silence — there is nothing to switch off.
The full disclaimer

ECHO.11 is a self-care tool, not a medical device, and nothing here is medical advice. This session is not a diagnosis, a treatment, or a substitute for care from a doctor or sleep specialist. If you regularly struggle to fall or stay asleep for weeks at a time, talk to a doctor: the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia is a structured therapy called CBT-I, not a sound. Loud snoring, gasping at night or heavy daytime sleepiness are also worth a doctor's visit.

Response varies from person to person, and some people notice nothing at all. That is a normal outcome, not a failure on your part.

Keep the volume low and the phone at arm's length rather than under the pillow. Don't use it while driving. If the sound keeps you awake instead, turn it off — silence is a perfectly good way to fall asleep.

The research described on this page reports what studies have observed in general — not what will happen to you, and no efficacy figures are claimed for this specific recording.

This is not a crisis service. If you are in danger or thinking about harming yourself, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line right away.
