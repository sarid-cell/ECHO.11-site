What Are Gamma Waves?

Gamma waves are the fastest brain rhythms usually measured, roughly 30 to 100 cycles per second (30–100 Hz). They are very small on a scalp EEG, and muscle activity from the jaw and eyes falls in the same range, so they are hard to record cleanly. Researchers study gamma in connection with perception, attention and memory, and 40 Hz is the rate most often used in experiments.

What the band is

Gamma covers roughly 30 to 100 Hz, the fastest of the conventional bands. It is low in amplitude and hard to record cleanly from the scalp, because muscle activity from the jaw and eyes produces signal in the same range — a long-standing problem in the literature and a reason to read gamma findings carefully.

What gamma waves are linked to

Gamma synchrony has been proposed as a mechanism for binding separate features into a single percept — the temporal correlation hypothesis, reviewed by Singer and Gray (1995, Annual Review of Neuroscience)1. It is an influential hypothesis rather than a settled account.

In mouse models, 40 Hz sensory stimulation has been shown to drive gamma activity and to reduce amyloid load (Iaccarino and colleagues, 2016, Nature)2. That work used flickering light in a laboratory, in mice; human trials are ongoing and it says nothing about an audio track in headphones.

For attention specifically, the picture is mixed. Colzato, Barone, Sellaro and Hommel (2017, Psychological Research)3 reported effects on attentional focusing, while Reedijk, Bolders and Hommel (2013, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience)4 found the response depended on the listener's baseline state. So far, the evidence on focus is mixed.

Gamma in sleep and dreams

Gamma is often filed under “awake and focused”, but it also shows up at night. One review describes the brain running in two modes: a slow mode in deep sleep, with delta waves, and a fast mode in active waking and in REM sleep, with theta rhythm in the hippocampus and gamma across the rest of the cortex.6

In a 2014 sleep-lab study, a weak 40 Hz current applied to the front and sides of the head during REM raised reports of lucid dreaming, dreams in which you know you are dreaming. That was electrical stimulation, not sound, and a 40 Hz track has not been shown to do the same.7

No brainwave band is “the subconscious”. Gamma tracks active processing, and it is busiest when you are alert and when you dream.

What gamma waves are not

Gamma is not a cognitive enhancer you can switch on, and a 40 Hz audio session is not the MIT work. That research combines light and sound in a clinical context and is about disease pathology in animal models; this is an eleven-minute tone for a person trying to start a task.

It is also not a treatment for Alzheimer's disease, and nothing on this site should be read as suggesting otherwise.

Gamma waves FAQ

What are gamma waves?

Gamma waves are the fastest conventional EEG band, roughly 30 to 100 Hz. They are low in amplitude and hard to record cleanly, because jaw and eye muscle activity produces signal in the same range.

What do gamma waves do?

One influential idea, the temporal correlation hypothesis, proposes that gamma synchrony helps bind separate features, like the color and shape of an object, into a single percept. It is a hypothesis rather than a settled account.

What is a gamma surge?

The phrase comes from studies that recorded a sudden burst of gamma activity in the brain around the time of death, first in rats and then in people. A 2023 study in PNAS described it in two of four patients after life support was withdrawn. The cases are few and what the surge means is not known.8

Do gamma waves happen during sleep?

Yes, in REM sleep. In REM the brain runs in a fast mode, with theta rhythm in the hippocampus and gamma rhythm across the cortex, much as it does when you are awake and active. Deep sleep is the slow mode, with delta waves.6
